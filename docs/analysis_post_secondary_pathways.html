Dependent Variable

The cross-tab and ANOVA analysis exploring relationships between demographic, high school, and post-secondary variables with employment outcomes highlighted the importance of post-secondary variables in an individuals journey to meaningful employment inside and outside the region. Due to this, it makes sense to explore if some of the other variables play a role in post-secondary outcomes - whether an individual attends, graduates, the type of institution they attend and the location.

We will explore this here. We will explore this in three sections;

Tier 1: Core Postsecondary Access and Attainment

These outcomes are most foundational and closely tied to employment.

1. attended.ps.within.first.year.hsgrad

Question: Who transitions directly to postsecondary? Why it matters: Strongly linked to long-term success and minimizes “disconnection.”

2. attended.ps (ever attended postsecondary)

Question: Who attends postsecondary at all? Why it matters: Broader view than immediate enrollment.

3. ps.grad (graduated from postsecondary)

Question: Who completes a credential? Why it matters: Credential completion is often the real driver of employment outcomes.

🔹 Tier 2: Type of Postsecondary Experience

These outcomes differentiate the nature of the experience, which may shape career pathways and economic outcomes.

4. ps.grad.InstitutionSector

Question: Who graduates from 2-year vs. 4-year, public vs. private schools? Why it matters: This affects cost, prestige, job access, and transfer opportunities.

5. highest.cred.level

Question: Who earns certificates, associate degrees, bachelor’s degrees? Why it matters: Different credentials yield different labor market returns.

Tier 3: Location of Postsecondary Experience

These outcomes reveal mobility, local engagement, or place-based equity patterns.

6. ps.grad.location

Question: Who stays in the region for school? Why it matters: Tied to affordability and regional workforce planning.

Interpretation of findings

Immediate postsecondary enrollment within one year of high school graduation is shaped by a combination of individual, school-level, and academic preparation factors.

From a demographic perspective, the strongest influences were special education status and economic status. Students who had received special education services and those who were economically disadvantaged were substantially less likely to attend immediately, suggesting barriers related to academic support needs and financial resources. Race/ethnicity also played a role, with Asian/PI and White students having the highest rates and American Indian students the lowest, reflecting broader systemic disparities.

High school characteristics added another layer of influence. Economic conditions in the community—particularly lower unemployment rates and higher wages relative to the state average—were more strongly associated with immediate enrollment than rurality alone. While urban students had slightly higher attendance rates than their rural counterparts, community economic health appeared to be the more critical driver, potentially reflecting both the local opportunity structure and the resources available to schools and families.

High school accomplishments showed the most direct and consistent relationship with postsecondary entry. Strong academic performance (ACT and MCA scores) and early college exposure through PSEO were powerful predictors of immediate enrollment, with PSEO standing out as the single strongest individual factor. In contrast, greater engagement in Career and Technical Education was linked to lower immediate enrollment, likely reflecting alternative workforce-oriented pathways.

Taken together, these findings suggest that academic readiness, early exposure to college, and favorable economic contexts all facilitate rapid postsecondary transition, while financial, academic support, and systemic equity barriers can slow or divert that path. At the same time, CTE participation highlights that not all students aim for immediate college enrollment—emphasizing the importance of supporting multiple, equally viable pathways into adulthood.

Interpretation

Individual demographic characteristics showed meaningful differences in the likelihood of attending postsecondary within one year of high school graduation, with special education status and economic status having the strongest associations. Students who had received special education services and those who were economically disadvantaged were much less likely to make the immediate transition to postsecondary, highlighting potential barriers related to academic support needs and financial resources.

Race/ethnicity was also significantly related to postsecondary attendance, though the differences were smaller in magnitude. Asian/PI and White students had the highest immediate attendance rates, while American Indian students had the lowest. These patterns suggest disparities that may stem from a combination of historical, structural, and resource-related factors influencing access to and navigation of postsecondary pathways.

Taken together, the demographic findings indicate that academic support needs, economic resources, and systemic equity issues all play roles in shaping postsecondary access. Addressing these factors—through targeted supports, financial assistance, and culturally responsive programming—could help close gaps in immediate postsecondary enrollment.

Interpretation

High school context appears to play a meaningful, though varied, role in whether students attend postsecondary within one year of graduation. Community economic conditions—both unemployment rate and wage levels relative to the state average—were more strongly linked to postsecondary attendance than RUCA classification (urban–rural mix). Students from economically stronger areas (lower unemployment, higher wages) were more likely to transition quickly to postsecondary and to complete within the first year.

In contrast, the urban–rural mix of a high school’s location showed only a weak relationship with postsecondary attendance. While entirely urban schools had the highest immediate attendance rates and town/rural mix schools the lowest, the gap was relatively small compared to the economic variables.

These results suggest that economic opportunity in the community where a student attends high school may be a more important driver of immediate postsecondary enrollment and completion than rurality alone. However, the weaker relationship with RUCA also indicates that place still matters, but its effects may operate indirectly—perhaps through economic conditions or access to postsecondary institutions—rather than through rurality itself.

Interpretation

High school accomplishment measures reveal two distinct patterns in immediate postsecondary enrollment. First, indicators of college readiness and academic achievement—such as higher ACT scores, stronger MCA performance in math, reading, and science, and participation in PSEO—are strongly linked to higher rates of attending and completing postsecondary within one year. The particularly strong relationship with PSEO suggests that early exposure to college coursework not only signals readiness but may also build momentum toward rapid postsecondary transition.

Second, greater involvement in Career and Technical Education (CTE)—both in terms of number of courses taken and concentrator/completer status—was associated with lower immediate postsecondary attendance. This may reflect intentional pathway choices, where students prepare for direct workforce entry rather than college, or differences in academic preparation and postsecondary aspirations.

Overall, these findings indicate that college-oriented academic preparation supports immediate postsecondary entry, while career-oriented pathways may divert graduates toward alternative post–high school options. This divergence highlights the importance of aligning supports with students’ chosen pathways—ensuring that those pursuing workforce entry have strong employment prospects, while those aiming for college receive the preparation and early exposure that promotes successful transition.
