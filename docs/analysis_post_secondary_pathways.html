Dependent Variable

The cross-tab and ANOVA analysis exploring relationships between demographic, high school, and post-secondary variables with employment outcomes highlighted the importance of post-secondary variables in an individuals journey to meaningful employment inside and outside the region. Due to this, it makes sense to explore if some of the other variables play a role in post-secondary outcomes - whether an individual attends, graduates, the type of institution they attend and the location.

We will explore this here. We will explore this in three sections;

Tier 1: Core Postsecondary Access and Attainment

These outcomes are most foundational and closely tied to employment.

1. attended.ps.within.first.year.hsgrad

Question: Who transitions directly to postsecondary? Why it matters: Strongly linked to long-term success and minimizes “disconnection.”

2. attended.ps (ever attended postsecondary)

Question: Who attends postsecondary at all? Why it matters: Broader view than immediate enrollment.

3. ps.grad (graduated from postsecondary)

Question: Who completes a credential? Why it matters: Credential completion is often the real driver of employment outcomes.

🔹 Tier 2: Type of Postsecondary Experience

These outcomes differentiate the nature of the experience, which may shape career pathways and economic outcomes.

4. ps.grad.InstitutionSector

Question: Who graduates from 2-year vs. 4-year, public vs. private schools? Why it matters: This affects cost, prestige, job access, and transfer opportunities.

5. highest.cred.level

Question: Who earns certificates, associate degrees, bachelor’s degrees? Why it matters: Different credentials yield different labor market returns.

Tier 3: Location of Postsecondary Experience

These outcomes reveal mobility, local engagement, or place-based equity patterns.

6. ps.grad.location

Question: Who stays in the region for school? Why it matters: Tied to affordability and regional workforce planning.

Interpretation of findings

Immediate postsecondary enrollment within one year of high school graduation is shaped by a combination of individual, school-level, and academic preparation factors.

From a demographic perspective, the strongest influences were special education status and economic status. Students who had received special education services and those who were economically disadvantaged were substantially less likely to attend immediately, suggesting barriers related to academic support needs and financial resources. Race/ethnicity also played a role, with Asian/PI and White students having the highest rates and American Indian students the lowest, reflecting broader systemic disparities.

High school characteristics added another layer of influence. Economic conditions in the community—particularly lower unemployment rates and higher wages relative to the state average—were more strongly associated with immediate enrollment than rurality alone. While urban students had slightly higher attendance rates than their rural counterparts, community economic health appeared to be the more critical driver, potentially reflecting both the local opportunity structure and the resources available to schools and families.

High school accomplishments showed the most direct and consistent relationship with postsecondary entry. Strong academic performance (ACT and MCA scores) and early college exposure through PSEO were powerful predictors of immediate enrollment, with PSEO standing out as the single strongest individual factor. In contrast, greater engagement in Career and Technical Education was linked to lower immediate enrollment, likely reflecting alternative workforce-oriented pathways.

Taken together, these findings suggest that academic readiness, early exposure to college, and favorable economic contexts all facilitate rapid postsecondary transition, while financial, academic support, and systemic equity barriers can slow or divert that path. At the same time, CTE participation highlights that not all students aim for immediate college enrollment—emphasizing the importance of supporting multiple, equally viable pathways into adulthood.

Rank of variable importance

  1. PSEO participation
    • Why: Strongest effect size (Cramer’s V=0.356) and largest differences in immediate postsecondary attendance (81.4% vs. 46.8% for non-participants).
    • Interpretation: Clear indicator of college readiness and momentum toward rapid postsecondary entry.
  2. Special education status
    • Why: Second-highest effect size (Cramer’s V=0.282) with a large gap in attendance (67.9% vs. 29.5%).
    • Interpretation: Indicates substantial barriers for students with special education needs.
  3. ACT Composite Score
    • Why: Very large F-value (2827.0) and substantial mean score differences between groups (up to 3 points).
    • Interpretation: Strong measure of academic readiness and predictor of immediate enrollment.
  4. Economic status
    • Why: Moderate effect size (Cramer’s V=0.225) and large gap (70% vs. 46.7%).
    • Interpretation: Reflects the role of financial resources in enabling postsecondary transition.
  5. MCA scores (Math, Reading, Science)
    • Why: All had very large F-values (4,000–6,000+) with consistent gaps favoring completers.
    • Interpretation: Reinforces that academic performance across core subjects correlates with postsecondary entry.
  6. Average county unemployment rate
    • Why: Largest F-value among school characteristics (575.6) and clear differences in economic contexts between completers and non-completers.
    • Interpretation: Suggests broader labor market conditions may influence postsecondary decisions.
  7. Average wages (percent of state)
    • Why: Strong F-value (324.7) and consistent pattern of higher wages linked to higher attendance.
    • Interpretation: Captures local economic strength and opportunity.
  8. Race/Ethnicity
    • Why: Smaller effect size (Cramer’s V=0.113) but notable disparities across groups.
    • Interpretation: Points to systemic inequities that affect postsecondary access.
  9. CTE achievement level
    • Why: Weak association (Cramer’s V=0.071) but reverse pattern—higher involvement linked to lower immediate enrollment.
    • Interpretation: Likely reflects intentional workforce-oriented pathways.
  10. Total CTE courses taken
    • Why: Large F-value (1002.0) but consistent with CTE achievement in direction—more CTE linked to lower immediate enrollment.
    • Interpretation: Reinforces the CTE–workforce pathway pattern.
  11. RUCA classification
    • Why: Weakest association (Cramer’s V=0.035) and small differences in rates.
    • Interpretation: Rurality has limited direct impact once other factors are considered, though it may operate indirectly through economic conditions and access.

Individual demographic characteristics showed meaningful differences in the likelihood of attending postsecondary within one year of high school graduation, with special education status and economic status having the strongest associations. Taken together, the demographic findings indicate that academic support needs, economic resources, and systemic equity issues all play roles in shaping postsecondary access. Addressing these factors—through targeted supports, financial assistance, and culturally responsive programming—could help close gaps in immediate postsecondary enrollment.

Interpretation

High school context appears to play a meaningful, though varied, role in whether students attend postsecondary within one year of graduation. Community economic conditions—both unemployment rate and wage levels relative to the state average—were more strongly linked to postsecondary attendance than RUCA classification (urban–rural mix). Students from economically stronger areas (lower unemployment, higher wages) were more likely to transition quickly to postsecondary and to complete within the first year.

In contrast, the urban–rural mix of a high school’s location showed only a weak relationship with postsecondary attendance. While entirely urban schools had the highest immediate attendance rates and town/rural mix schools the lowest, the gap was relatively small compared to the economic variables.

These results suggest that economic opportunity in the community where a student attends high school may be a more important driver of immediate postsecondary enrollment and completion than rurality alone. However, the weaker relationship with RUCA also indicates that place still matters, but its effects may operate indirectly—perhaps through economic conditions or access to postsecondary institutions—rather than through rurality itself.

Interpretation

High school accomplishment measures reveal two distinct patterns in immediate postsecondary enrollment. First, indicators of college readiness and academic achievement—such as higher ACT scores, stronger MCA performance in math, reading, and science, and participation in PSEO—are strongly linked to higher rates of attending and completing postsecondary within one year. The particularly strong relationship with PSEO suggests that early exposure to college coursework not only signals readiness but may also build momentum toward rapid postsecondary transition.

Second, greater involvement in Career and Technical Education (CTE)—both in terms of number of courses taken and concentrator/completer status—was associated with lower immediate postsecondary attendance. This may reflect intentional pathway choices, where students prepare for direct workforce entry rather than college, or differences in academic preparation and postsecondary aspirations.

Interpretation of findings

Postsecondary participation at any point after high school is influenced by a combination of individual demographic factors, the economic and geographic context of the high school, and academic preparation and achievement during high school.

From a demographic perspective, special education status and economic status are the strongest predictors of whether a student ever attends postsecondary. Students who had received special education services and those who were economically disadvantaged were substantially less likely to attend, underscoring the role of resource availability and academic support needs in shaping postsecondary trajectories. Race/ethnicity differences, though smaller in magnitude, still point to systemic inequities, with American Indian students having the lowest participation rates and Asian/PI and White students the highest.

High school characteristics contribute to postsecondary participation, though their influence is more modest compared to demographics and accomplishments. Students from high schools in communities with higher wages relative to the state average were more likely to attend, suggesting a link between local economic prosperity and access to postsecondary education. The small positive relationship with unemployment rate may indicate that fewer local job opportunities encourage more students to seek education beyond high school. Rurality, as measured by RUCA classification, showed only a weak association, suggesting that economic context outweighs location alone in determining eventual postsecondary participation.

High school accomplishments remain the most direct and consistent predictors. Higher ACT and MCA scores in all tested subjects, and especially participation in PSEO, were strongly associated with eventual postsecondary attendance. PSEO participation stands out as the single most powerful academic-related factor, suggesting that early exposure to college coursework creates momentum toward enrollment, even if students delay entry. In contrast, greater involvement in Career and Technical Education—both in number of courses and concentrator/completer status—was linked to lower postsecondary participation, likely reflecting intentional workforce entry pathways.

Taken together, these findings suggest that eventual postsecondary participation is shaped by a complex interplay of personal resources, community context, and academic preparation. Strengthening supports for economically disadvantaged students, students receiving special education services, and underrepresented racial/ethnic groups—while sustaining and expanding opportunities like PSEO—may be key to increasing postsecondary access. At the same time, supporting the success of students pursuing career-oriented high school pathways will be essential to ensuring equitable long-term employment outcomes.

Rank of variable importance

The relative importance of most predictors remained stable when comparing immediate postsecondary attendance to ever attending postsecondary. PSEO participation and special education status consistently ranked as the top two predictors, followed closely by ACT Composite Score. Academic achievement measures (MCA Reading, Math, and Science) maintained strong positions in both timeframes.

Two notable shifts emerged: Total CTE courses rose in importance for ever attendance (ranked 11th to 8th), while average county unemployment rate declined (8th to 11th). These changes suggest that CTE involvement may play a slightly larger role in eventual enrollment than in immediate enrollment, while local unemployment conditions appear less influential over longer time horizons.

Overall, academic readiness, early exposure to college coursework, and key demographic factors remain the most consistent drivers of postsecondary participation, regardless of timing, while some contextual factors shift in influence over time.

Interpretation of findings

When considering all variables together, the largest associations with postsecondary graduation were found in high school accomplishments and demographics, with high school characteristics showing weaker but still significant effects.

Top associations emerged for:

  • PSEO participation (φ = 0.370) – very strong positive association.
  • Race/Ethnicity (Cramer’s V = 0.271) – substantial differences across groups.
  • Special Education status (φ = 0.243) – strong negative association.
  • Economic status (φ = 0.231) – lower-income students had lower graduation rates.
  • ACT Composite Score (Δ = +2.95) – large academic performance gap.

Moderate associations included:

  • MCA scores (Math, Reading, Science: Δ ≈ +0.47 to +0.54).
  • CTE achievement (Cramer’s V = 0.149) – completers more likely to graduate.
  • Total CTE courses (Δ = −1.13) – inverse relationship.

Weaker associations appeared in:

  • High school unemployment rate (Δ = +0.17) – moderate effect.
  • Rural-urban classification (Cramer’s V = 0.033) – very small effect.
  • High school average wage level (Δ = +0.005) – minimal practical effect.

Academic readiness and early postsecondary exposure dominate as predictors of postsecondary graduation. PSEO participation stands out as the single strongest correlate, followed closely by ACT performance and MCA scores, signaling that preparation for college-level work during high school is a decisive factor in persistence and completion.

Demographic variables also show meaningful influence: disparities by race/ethnicity, economic status, and special education status indicate that systemic barriers and resource gaps persist well into postsecondary pathways.

High school community context—such as unemployment rates—matters, but less so than individual-level academic factors. The weak relationship with rurality and average wage levels suggests that broader geographic classifications do not strongly dictate completion outcomes once students are enrolled.

CTE pathways present a nuanced picture: while achieving concentrator/completer status correlates with higher graduation rates, a greater volume of CTE coursework is linked to lower completion, perhaps indicating divergence toward employment-focused rather than degree-focused trajectories.

Overall, the data point to a layered structure of influence, with academic preparation and early college engagement as the most actionable levers, demographic disparities as structural challenges, and community context as a secondary but notable factor.

  • Economic Status – Strongly differentiates sectors, with lower-income students overrepresented in Public 2-year institutions and higher-income students in Public 4-year institutions.
  • Special Education Status – Strong association; students with special education experience are much more likely to graduate from Public 2-year institutions.
  • ACT Composite Score – Largest academic gap across sectors; 4-year graduates score highest, 2-year lowest, “Other” in between.
  • PSEO Participation – Significantly higher among Public 4-year graduates, lowest in Public 2-year sector.
  • CTE Achievement – Strong, inverse pattern to PSEO; highest among Public 2-year graduates, lowest in Public 4-year sector.

Moderate Associations

  • Race/Ethnicity – Distinct group distributions, with White and Asian/Pacific Islander graduates concentrated in the 4-year sector, and Black and Hispanic graduates more prevalent in Public 2-year and “Other” sectors.

  • Total CTE Courses Taken – Higher among Public 2-year graduates, lower in Public 4-year graduates, mirroring CTE achievement patterns.

  • Average Wages (% of State Median) – Similar pattern to unemployment rate; statistically significant but minor differences across sectors.

The sector of graduation reflects a sorting process shaped most strongly by socioeconomic status, academic preparation, and high school program participation. High-income, academically strong students with greater exposure to advanced coursework (e.g., PSEO) are more likely to graduate from Public 4-year institutions. Students from lower-income households or with stronger vocational preparation through CTE tend toward Public 2-year institutions. Demographic background and local economic conditions matter, but less so than direct academic and programmatic experiences.

Interpretation Across All Analyses

Your analyses across attended.ps.within.first.year.hsgrad, attended.ps, ps.grad, ps.grad.location, and ps.grad.InstitutionSector converge on a clear story: postsecondary pathways, especially the type and location of college attendance and graduation, strongly shape long-term employment outcomes.

1. Academic preparation is rising as a key differentiator.

The most striking finding is the surge in ACT Composite Score as a top predictor for where and what type of institution students graduate from. This shift suggests that academic readiness now plays a bigger role than family income in determining postsecondary patterns, especially when looking beyond “did they graduate” to “where did they graduate from.”

For workforce engagement, this implies:

  • Students with higher academic readiness are more likely to leave the local region (out-of-state or outside their high school region) and attend 4-year institutions — potentially making them harder to retain locally.
  • Local workforce strategies need to consider how to connect academically strong students to in-region career opportunities early, before they are “pulled away” by academic mobility.

2. Economic status still matters — but differently now.

In earlier models (overall participation and graduation), Economic Status was consistently the top predictor. In the location- and sector-based analyses, it dropped several spots.

  • This means income is less about whether students succeed in postsecondary education, and more about the type of postsecondary pathway they choose.
  • Lower-income students are more concentrated in public 2-year colleges — which tend to be closer to home and linked to local employment pipelines.

For workforce planning:

  • Public 2-year pathways are a prime engagement point for students from lower-income backgrounds — this is where targeted partnerships, apprenticeships, and “earn while you learn” models could have high impact.

3. Local economic conditions influence mobility decisions.

Average Unemployment Rate rose in importance for ps.grad.location and ps.grad.InstitutionSector, suggesting that students’ decisions about where to attend/graduate are shaped by job market health.

  • Higher unemployment in home regions may push students toward other areas for school — and potentially for work afterward.
  • Conversely, strong local economies may retain more students in local or regional institutions.

This underscores the importance of making local job opportunities visible to students during high school, not just after graduation.

4. CTE and PSEO matter more for pathway type than for location retention.

Career & Technical Education (CTE) and PSEO participation have moderate influence in sector-based models, but remain lower-ranked overall.

  • PSEO tends to correlate with 4-year public graduation, while CTE is more evenly distributed but strongest in 2-year institutions.
  • These programs may be less about keeping students local and more about shaping the kind of postsecondary credential they earn.

The implication:

  • To retain students locally, align CTE pathways with local industry demand and connect PSEO experiences to in-region internships or mentorships.

5. The mobility-retention challenge is real.

Across analyses, a pattern emerges:

  • High academic readiness + higher income → higher likelihood of out-of-state or outside-region graduation (and thus risk of workforce loss).
  • Lower income + CTE focus → higher likelihood of 2-year local or regional graduation (and thus stronger potential for workforce retention).

If the goal is to engage high school students in the local workforce, the strategies may need to be dual-tracked:

  1. Retention Track: Strengthen the connection between 2-year programs and local employers, with pathways that lead to strong wages and advancement without needing to leave the area.
  2. Return Track: For students likely to leave (high ACT, 4-year, out-of-state), create strong alumni networks, internships, and “boomerang” incentives to bring them back post-graduation.

Bottom line:

This analysis confirms that postsecondary type and location are the levers that matter most for workforce engagement. Academic readiness now rivals — and in some cases surpasses — family income as a driver of where students go after high school. Local workforce strategy should therefore focus on making both academic and career pathways visible, valuable, and connected to the region — with different engagement tactics for students on different trajectories.
