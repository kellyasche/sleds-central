Summary of Analysis

Analysis 1: Workforce Participation Outcomes and Associated Factors

What this analysis examined

This analysis examined how four groups of factors — demographics, high school characteristics (place-based factors), high school accomplishments, and postsecondary experiences — are associated with workforce participation outcomes 1, 5, and 10 years after high school graduation.

Outcomes include:

  • Meaningful employment in the Central region (1,000+ hours with one employer in a calendar year)
  • Meaningful employment elsewhere in Minnesota
  • Not meaningful employment
  • No Minnesota employment record
  • Postsecondary enrollment (at early time points)

The central policy question is: What factors are most associated with graduates finding and maintaining meaningful employment in the Central region — whether or not they attend college?

Major findings

1. Completing a postsecondary credential in the region is one of the strongest long-term predictors of meaningful regional employment.

By Years 5 and 10, graduates who complete a credential in the region are substantially more likely to be in meaningful regional employment than those completing credentials elsewhere.

Credential location appears to function as a regional anchoring mechanism.

However, this is not the only pathway into meaningful regional employment.

2. Associate degree pathways appear more regionally anchored than bachelor’s pathways.

At Year 5:

  • Associate degree holders: approximately 29–30% are in meaningful regional employment.
  • Bachelor’s degree holders: more likely to be in meaningful employment elsewhere in Minnesota (approximately 27%).

This suggests that credential type — especially two-year degrees that may align more directly with regional labor demand — is strongly tied to staying local.

3. Non-college pathways can and do lead to meaningful regional employment — but early attachment is more fragile.

Graduates without postsecondary credentials still appear in meaningful regional employment categories, especially by Years 5 and 10.

However, at Year 1:

  • Approximately 46% of non-credentialed graduates are in not meaningful employment.
  • Approximately 28% have no MN employment record.

Some individuals transition into stable regional employment over time, but the early years show substantially higher risk of weak labor market attachment.

This suggests that workforce-connected non-college pathways matter — but require strong employer alignment and early labor market integration.

4. CTE achievement supports early workforce attachment, particularly for workforce-first pathways.

CTE achievement (not simply taking CTE courses) shows statistically significant associations with workforce outcomes at Year 1 and modest associations at Year 5.

  • Achievement-based CTE signals are more predictive than total CTE course counts.
  • CTE appears associated with stronger early labor market attachment and lower early disconnection risk.

However, in this full-population model, CTE does not rival credential completion location in long-term regional sorting power.

Its influence is strongest in the early transition years and likely operates most clearly among students who move directly into workforce pathways rather than four-year degree pathways.

5. High school academic engagement signals are strong early indicators.

At Year 1:

  • ACT participation: Cramér’s V ≈ 0.42
  • PSEO participation: V ≈ 0.36

These signals are associated with higher rates of postsecondary enrollment and meaningful employment outcomes. Their influence declines over time but remains detectable.

Depth of engagement (achievement indicators) matters more than simple participation counts.

6. Regional economic context consistently shapes outcomes.

High school Economic Development Region shows small but stable associations (V ≈ 0.07–0.08 across years).

County wage context is strongly associated with pathway outcomes at every time point (F-statistics approximately 86–103, p < .001).

Students from stronger wage contexts are more likely to be in meaningful employment or postsecondary pathways.

Structural opportunity matters alongside individual preparation.

Overall interpretation

Meaningful employment in the Central region emerges from multiple pathways.

One pathway runs through regionally completed postsecondary credentials — especially associate degrees aligned with local labor demand.

Another pathway runs directly from high school into the workforce. This pathway can lead to meaningful regional employment, but early attachment is more fragile and requires strong workforce alignment.

CTE achievement appears to strengthen early workforce connection, particularly for workforce-first students, but it does not by itself anchor long-term regional retention in the way credential completion location does.

Bachelor’s degree pathways are more geographically mobile and more frequently associated with meaningful employment elsewhere in Minnesota.

The strongest and most durable regional retention pattern is tied to where credentials are completed, not simply whether someone attends college.

At the same time, high school engagement and local economic context both matter. Workforce retention is shaped by preparation, opportunity structure, and alignment between education pathways and regional labor demand.

Retention is not one pathway — it is an ecosystem.


Analysis 2: What Shapes Postsecondary Pathways?

What this analysis examined

This phase of the analysis examined which demographic characteristics, high school context factors, and high school accomplishments are associated with:

  • Graduating from postsecondary (ps.grad)
  • The type of credential earned (2-year vs. 4-year; public vs. private)
  • The location of graduation (in-region, elsewhere in Minnesota, or out-of-state)

These outcomes matter because earlier findings showed that credential completion — and especially where that credential is completed — strongly shape long-term workforce location and attachment.

The goal here is to identify what upstream factors influence completion, credential type, and geographic mobility in postsecondary education.

Major findings

1. Postsecondary completion is driven primarily by academic readiness and structural barriers.

The strongest predictors of graduating from postsecondary are:

  • PSEO participation (φ ≈ 0.370)
  • Race/Ethnicity (Cramér’s V ≈ 0.271)
  • Special education status (φ ≈ 0.243)
  • Economic status (φ ≈ 0.231)
  • ACT Composite Score (≈ 3-point average gap between graduates and non-graduates)

Completion gaps are large and persistent:

  • Students receiving special education services graduated at 13.4%, compared to 47.1% for non-special education peers.
  • Economically disadvantaged students graduated at 25.9%, compared to 50.1% for higher-income peers.
  • American Indian students had the lowest graduation rates (15.8%), while Asian/PI students had the highest (43.7%).

Completion is not evenly distributed. It is strongly shaped by preparation and structural support conditions.

2. Credential type reflects academic preparation and mobility orientation.

Academic performance measures (ACT and MCA scores) strongly differentiate institution sector.

Students graduating from 4-year institutions show higher academic performance than those graduating from 2-year institutions, who in turn outperform non-completers.

This pattern suggests:

  • Higher academic readiness increases the likelihood of 4-year degree attainment.
  • 2-year pathways serve as an important bridge for students with moderate academic preparation.
  • Non-completion is more concentrated among students facing academic and resource barriers.

Credential type is not random — it aligns closely with preparation and opportunity.

3. Graduation location is most strongly shaped by academic achievement and economic context.

When examining where students graduate:

  • ACT and MCA scores are the strongest differentiators.
  • Out-of-state graduates have the highest academic performance.
  • In-state but out-of-region graduates fall in the middle.
  • In-region graduates tend to have lower average academic scores.

Economic status also plays a meaningful role:

  • Higher-income students are more likely to graduate outside their home region.
  • Lower-income students are more likely to remain in-region.

This suggests that academic readiness increases geographic mobility, while economic constraints increase geographic anchoring.

4. CTE plays a secondary but directional role.

CTE achievement shows a moderate association with postsecondary graduation (V ≈ 0.149).

However:

  • Greater CTE involvement is associated with lower rates of 4-year degree attainment.
  • It aligns more closely with 2-year pathways or workforce-first trajectories.

CTE does not emerge as a primary predictor of completion location, but rather as a pathway signal indicating different postsecondary intentions.

5. Community context has limited independent influence.

Rural–urban classification and average local wage context show statistically significant but comparatively weak associations.

In rank-order comparisons, community-level variables consistently fall below student-level academic and demographic predictors.

Individual preparation and structural barriers outweigh geography alone in determining completion and mobility patterns.

Overall interpretation

Postsecondary completion and graduation location — the two strongest downstream drivers of workforce attachment — are shaped primarily by academic readiness and structural support conditions.

Three broad patterns emerge:

First, academic preparation drives mobility. Higher-performing students are more likely to earn 4-year degrees and graduate outside their home region, increasing geographic mobility.

Second, economic and special education barriers significantly reduce completion rates and constrain options.

Third, in-region graduation is more common among students with lower academic performance and lower income — suggesting that regional retention is partly a function of mobility constraints, not just regional opportunity.

For regional leaders, this has major implications:

  • If long-term workforce retention depends on regional credential completion, then strengthening regional postsecondary capacity must include academic preparation pipelines.
  • If the goal is to retain high-achieving students, competitive regional 4-year opportunities and strong career alignment are critical.
  • If equity is a priority, addressing completion gaps for economically disadvantaged and special education students is essential.
  • If workforce-first pathways are central, CTE must be paired with strong employer-connected postsecondary bridges.

Completion and location are not accidental outcomes. They are shaped early — by readiness, resources, and opportunity structures — and those choices cascade into long-term regional workforce patterns.


Analysis 3: Multivariate Confirmation Analysis – Logistic Regression

What this analysis examined

This phase uses logistic regression to confirm which variables independently predict:

  • Sustained employment – region (meaningful employment in the Central region vs. all other workforce outcomes)
  • Postsecondary graduation – region (graduated from a postsecondary institution in the region vs. graduated elsewhere or did not complete)

These models test whether the strongest associations identified in earlier bivariate analyses remain significant once key variables are considered simultaneously.

Major findings

Sustained Employment – Region Model

1. Postsecondary sector × graduation location is the strongest predictor by a large margin.

Compared to all other pathways:

  • 2-year public × Regional graduation → OR ≈ 3.07 (~207% higher odds)
  • 4-year public × Regional graduation → OR ≈ 2.42 (~142% higher odds)
  • Other sector × Regional graduation → OR ≈ 1.70 (~70% higher odds)

Graduating outside the region substantially reduces the odds of sustained regional employment:

  • 4-year public outside MN → ~61% lower odds
  • Other sector outside MN → ~62% lower odds
  • 2-year public outside MN → ~41% lower odds

Credential location and institutional type dominate this model.

2. Structural disparities remain strong independent predictors.

Even after controlling for postsecondary pathway:

  • Black students → ~49% lower odds
  • Asian/PI students → ~36% lower odds
  • American Indian students → ~30% lower odds
  • Hispanic students → ~21% lower odds
  • Entirely urban high school context → ~47% lower odds
  • Economic disadvantage → ~9% lower odds

These effects remain statistically significant and are not explained away by credential pathway.

3. CTE participation retains independent significance.

  • CTE concentrators/completers → ~12% higher odds
  • CTE participants → ~11% higher odds

CTE remains positively associated with sustained regional employment even after controlling for credential level and graduation location.

4. Academic mobility indicators attenuate or reverse.

Variables that showed strong bivariate associations lose independent strength:

  • PSEO participation → not statistically significant in final workforce model
  • AP participation → ~30% lower odds
  • Higher MCA Math → modest reduction (~3% per unit increase)

Academic achievement influences pathway selection but does not independently anchor regional workforce retention once pathway is controlled.

Postsecondary Graduation – Region Model

1. Local economic context is one of the strongest positive predictors.

  • Higher average county wages (percent of state) → OR ≈ 1.87 (~87% higher odds)

Stronger local wage environments are strongly associated with graduating from a postsecondary institution within the region.

2. PSEO participation is a major independent positive predictor.

  • PSEO participants → OR ≈ 1.56 (~56% higher odds)

Early exposure to college coursework substantially increases the likelihood of completing postsecondary within the region.

3. Structural disparities strongly reduce the likelihood of in-region graduation.

  • Hispanic students → ~53% lower odds
  • American Indian students → ~43% lower odds
  • Asian/PI students → ~25% lower odds
  • Economic disadvantage → significantly lower odds
  • Special Education status → significantly lower odds

These effects remain even after controlling for academic preparation.

4. Academic readiness predicts mobility rather than regional anchoring.

Higher ACT and MCA scores are associated with lower likelihood of graduating in-region and greater likelihood of graduating outside the region.

Academic performance operates in a mobility direction.

5. CTE plays a limited role in graduation location.

CTE achievement does not emerge as a dominant independent predictor of graduating in-region once academic and demographic variables are controlled.

Its effect is substantially smaller than PSEO and wage context in this model.

Variables that weakened relative to earlier analyses

Several predictors that appeared strong in bivariate analyses are attenuated in multivariate models:

  • ACT and MCA scores (large bivariate effects; mobility-driven in multivariate models)
  • PSEO participation (strong in bivariate workforce analysis; operates through graduation pathway)
  • High school wage context (strong ANOVA differences in earlier phases; remains strong for graduation location but not dominant in workforce model)
  • Some school location variables (reduced once pathway and demographic factors included)

This indicates that many academic indicators operate indirectly through postsecondary sorting mechanisms.

Overall interpretation

The multivariate confirmation analysis clarifies two independent but connected mechanisms.

First, sustained regional employment is overwhelmingly structured by where and what type of postsecondary credential is completed. Regional credential completion — especially through 2-year public institutions — is the strongest predictor of long-term regional workforce attachment.

Second, regional postsecondary graduation is independently shaped by:

  • Local economic strength (wage context)
  • Early college exposure (PSEO participation)
  • Structural inequality (race, economic disadvantage, special education status)
  • Academic mobility patterns

Academic readiness increases geographic mobility in postsecondary completion.
Local economic ecosystems increase the likelihood of regional completion.
Structural disparities reduce both regional graduation and regional workforce attachment.

CTE operates as a workforce-alignment mechanism in the employment model, but does not independently drive regional college completion.

The confirmation models reinforce earlier findings while clarifying mechanism:

  • Postsecondary geography drives regional workforce retention.
  • Local economic context and PSEO strongly influence regional graduation.
  • Academic achievement predicts mobility.
  • Structural disparities persist across both models.


Analysis 4: Workforce Participation Among Non–Postsecondary Graduates

What this analysis examined

This phase restricts the sample to individuals who never completed a postsecondary credential and examines how demographic characteristics, high school context, and high school accomplishments are associated with workforce participation outcomes at 1, 5, and 10 years after graduation.

The workforce outcomes include:

  • Meaningful employment – region
  • Meaningful employment – MN (outside the region)
  • Not meaningful employment
  • No MN employment record

Cramér’s V statistics and ANOVA comparisons were used to identify the strength and direction of associations. This analysis isolates the workforce-only pathway, removing postsecondary completion as the dominant sorting mechanism.

Major findings

1. CTE course-taking is the strongest and most consistent workforce anchor for non-college graduates.

Across all three time points, CTE exposure shows a statistically significant association with workforce outcomes.

  • Year 1: χ² = 707.6, Cramér’s V = 0.074 (small but meaningful)
  • Year 5: association persists, effect size modest
  • Year 10: F(3, 27,144) = 33.7, p < .001 (differences remain present)

Year 1 patterns:

  • CTE Concentrators/Completors: 20.9% meaningful regional employment
  • CTE Participants: 17.6%
  • No CTE: 14.1%
  • No MN record highest among no CTE (20.3%)

Students with heavier CTE exposure were far more likely to secure meaningful employment (regional and statewide) and less likely to be disconnected.

By Year 10:

  • Students with no MN record had ~0.46 fewer CTE courses than those in meaningful regional employment.
  • Differences between meaningful regional and meaningful MN employment were negligible.

CTE provides a durable—though modest—advantage, especially in early workforce entry.

2. Special education status is one of the strongest early barriers.

Special education status shows one of the largest demographic effect sizes:

  • Year 1: Cramér’s V = 0.166 (moderate)
  • Year 5: 0.096
  • Year 10: 0.075

Year 1:

  • Special ed students: 40.4% non-meaningful work, 24.9% no MN record
  • Non-special ed: 32.9% non-meaningful, 15.5% no MN record

By Year 10:

  • Regional meaningful employment rates nearly equal (23.8% vs. 23.6%)
  • However, special ed students remain more concentrated in non-meaningful work and disconnection.

Early disparities are substantial and narrow over time for regional employment, but broader opportunity gaps persist.

3. Academic indicators show mobility patterns rather than regional anchoring.

ACT scores function differently in this restricted sample:

  • Lower ACT scorers are more likely to remain in regional employment.
  • Higher ACT scorers are more likely to have no MN employment record, suggesting mobility out of state or delayed workforce entry.

The influence of academic performance fades over time. By Year 10, individual-level academic indicators are far less predictive than economic context.

4. Local economic conditions increasingly dominate over time.

Structural labor market conditions become more influential the longer graduates are out of high school.

Key patterns:

  • Lower unemployment strengthens the likelihood of meaningful regional employment.
  • Higher unemployment increases the probability of no MN record.
  • Wage context interacts with unemployment:
    • Lower-wage but low-unemployment counties anchor regional employment.
    • Higher-wage or higher-unemployment contexts increase mobility or non-meaningful outcomes.

By Year 10, structural economic context outweighs most individual-level predictors.

5. Rural context acts as a regional anchor.

Urban–rural classification shows a statistically significant but modest association that strengthens over time:

  • Year 1: Cramér’s V = 0.060
  • Year 5: 0.090
  • Year 10: 0.099

More rural settings are associated with sustained regional employment. Urban/town mix areas show higher mobility or disconnection.

Place becomes more influential as time passes.

Overall interpretation

Among non-college graduates, workforce attachment is shaped by a combination of early preparation and structural labor market conditions.

At 1 year after graduation, CTE exposure is the clearest workforce anchor. Students with 3+ CTE courses are far more likely to secure meaningful regional employment, while those with little or no CTE are most at risk of disconnection.

By 5 years, the picture becomes more complex. Rural context, economic status, and special education status join CTE as key differentiators.

By 10 years, structural economic forces dominate. County wages and unemployment rates are the primary drivers of whether non-college graduates remain regionally employed or drift into non-meaningful work or disconnection. Individual-level academic indicators largely fade in influence.

Across all time horizons:

  • CTE provides a consistent protective effect.
  • Special education status presents an early and persistent vulnerability.
  • Lower academic performers are more regionally anchored.
  • Local labor demand ultimately determines long-term attachment.

For non-college graduates, meaningful regional employment is sustained when strong local labor demand intersects with high school workforce preparation — particularly CTE exposure — while structural barriers increase vulnerability to disconnection.


Analysis 5: Persistent Non-Meaningful Workforce Participation Among Non–Postsecondary Graduates

What this analysis examined

This phase moves beyond point-in-time workforce status and examines persistence of workforce disconnection among non-college graduates during the first five years after exiting formal education.

Individuals are classified annually as:

  • Meaningful workforce participation
  • Not meaningful workforce participation
  • No Minnesota employment record

Using these annual classifications, individuals are grouped based on persistence:

  • Intermittently disconnected: 1–2 of 5 years disconnected
  • Disconnected (broad): 3+ of 5 years disconnected
  • Persistently disconnected: 4–5 of 5 years disconnected

For years in which employment was not meaningful, location was further classified as in-region or outside-region to assess whether disconnection reflects regional underutilization or broader labor force detachment.

This approach shifts the focus from short-term instability to trajectory-based workforce patterns.

Major findings

1. Workforce disconnection is widespread and highly persistent.

More than half of non-college graduates are disconnected (not meaningful employment or no MN record) in at least four of the first five years after exiting formal education.

Intermittent disconnection is less common than sustained disconnection.

A relatively small share of non-college graduates remain consistently attached (meaningful employment in most or all five years). Stable early-career workforce integration is the exception rather than the norm.

This establishes that early disconnection is typically structural and enduring, not transitional.

2. Disconnection is primarily persistent rather than cyclical.

Trajectory analysis shows that individuals who experience early disconnection are unlikely to transition quickly into sustained meaningful employment.

Movement from repeated disconnection into consistent attachment occurs, but gradual recovery is more common than rapid stabilization.

Cycling between meaningful and non-meaningful employment is less frequent than extended periods of marginal attachment.

Early patterns tend to lock in.

3. Disconnection is often regional underutilization, not simply migration.

Among disconnected individuals:

  • When employment occurs, it is more often within Central Minnesota than elsewhere in the state.
  • Purely out-of-region marginal employment is less common than regional marginal employment.

This suggests that disconnection is not primarily a story of individuals leaving for better jobs, but of limited-quality regional job absorption.

The region retains many disconnected individuals — but often in unstable or low-intensity work.

4. Individual characteristics remain strong predictors of persistent disconnection.

Effect size comparisons show:

  • Special Education status is among the strongest predictors (Cramér’s V up to ~0.104 in earlier analyses).
  • Economic disadvantage compounds vulnerability.
  • Gender and EL status also show meaningful but smaller effects.

These individual-level characteristics outweigh geographic region in predictive strength.

5. Local economic context strongly shapes persistence.

Among high school characteristics:

  • County unemployment rate (ANOVA p < .001) shows the strongest association with the “attached vs. persistently disconnected” split.
  • Average county wage (percent of state) also significantly predicts extremes (persistent disconnection vs consistent attachment).
  • Lower unemployment and higher wages are associated with stable attachment.
  • Weaker wage environments increase persistent disconnection risk.

In contrast:

  • EDR (Cramér’s V ≈ 0.034) and
  • Rural/urban classification (Cramér’s V ≈ 0.026)

show very weak associations.

The “where” of region matters far less than the “strength” of the local economy.

6. CTE remains an important protective factor.

Across earlier non-college analyses and reinforced here:

  • Higher CTE course-taking is associated with stronger early workforce attachment.
  • Within weaker wage counties, students with 7+ CTE courses are more likely to remain meaningfully employed regionally.
  • Lower CTE exposure is associated with higher risk of persistent disconnection.

CTE does not eliminate structural economic constraints, but it buffers against them.

Overall interpretation

Among non-college graduates, workforce disconnection in the first five years after exiting formal education is not typically short-lived.

It is persistent.

More than half of this population experiences disconnection in four or more of their first five years. Intermittent instability exists, but long-term marginal attachment is far more common than rapid stabilization.

Disconnection is not primarily driven by regional flight. Many disconnected individuals remain in Central Minnesota but cycle through non-meaningful local employment or lack stable attachment altogether.

Predictors of persistent disconnection follow a clear hierarchy:

  1. Individual characteristics (especially Special Education status and economic disadvantage)
  2. Local economic strength (unemployment and wage levels)
  3. CTE engagement as a protective workforce-alignment mechanism
  4. Geographic region and rurality (comparatively weak effects)

The data indicate that for non-college graduates, early workforce instability tends to set a durable trajectory. Recovery is possible but uncommon without either strong individual preparation (e.g., CTE) or favorable local labor market conditions.

This phase reinforces a key structural insight:

Removing postsecondary completion as a sorting mechanism does not eliminate inequality. Instead, it reveals a workforce system in which local economic strength and early preparation determine whether non-college graduates become consistently attached or persistently disconnected.


Analysis 6: Multivariate Confirmation – Non–Postsecondary Graduates

What this analysis examined

This phase estimates logistic regression models predicting:

  • Sustained employment – region (meaningful employment in the Central region vs. all other workforce outcomes)

The sample is restricted to individuals who never completed a postsecondary credential.

Unlike earlier full-population models, no postsecondary pathway variables are included. The predictors are limited to:

  • Demographics
  • High school characteristics (rurality, school context, local wages, unemployment)
  • High school accomplishments (CTE, AP, PSEO, ACT, etc.)

The purpose is to identify which factors independently predict regional workforce attachment among non-college graduates when postsecondary completion is removed from the model.

Major findings

Year 1 – Early Workforce Attachment

1. CTE is the strongest positive predictor.

  • CTE Concentrator/Completor → OR ≈ 1.42 (~42% higher odds)
  • CTE Participant → OR ≈ 1.26 (~26% higher odds)

This is the largest positive effect in the model. Career-aligned coursework strongly predicts immediate regional workforce attachment.

2. Structural disparities are large and immediate.

  • American Indian students → OR ≈ 0.56 (~44% lower odds)
  • Asian/PI students → OR ≈ 0.61 (~39% lower odds)
  • Black students → OR ≈ 0.61 (~39% lower odds)
  • Hispanic students → OR ≈ 0.81 (~19% lower odds)
  • Special Education status → OR ≈ 0.71 (~29% lower odds)
  • Economic disadvantage → ~7% lower odds

These disparities appear at the first year and are not explained by preparation factors.

3. School context matters.

  • Entirely urban schools → OR ≈ 0.60 (~40% lower odds)
  • Urban/town/rural mix → OR ≈ 0.89 (~11% lower odds)

Students from more urban school contexts are substantially less likely to secure meaningful regional employment.

4. Academic acceleration does not promote regional retention.

  • AP participation → OR ≈ 0.80 (~20% lower odds)
  • PSEO participation → not statistically distinct

Academic acceleration aligns with outward mobility or alternative pathways rather than regional anchoring in this population.

Year 5 – Medium-Term Workforce Attachment

The structure of effects remains remarkably stable.

1. CTE continues as the strongest positive predictor.

  • CTE Concentrator/Completor → OR ≈ 1.22 (~22% higher odds)
  • CTE Participant → OR ≈ 1.23 (~23% higher odds)

Although slightly smaller than Year 1, CTE remains the dominant positive pathway into sustained regional employment.

2. Racial disparities persist and intensify.

  • Black students → OR ≈ 0.49 (~51% lower odds)
  • Asian/PI students → OR ≈ 0.49 (~51% lower odds)
  • American Indian students → OR ≈ 0.62 (~38% lower odds)
  • Hispanic students → OR ≈ 0.75 (~25% lower odds)
  • Special Education status → OR ≈ 0.88 (~12% lower odds)

Disparities remain substantial five years out.

3. Urban school context remains a structural correlate.

  • Entirely urban schools → OR ≈ 0.52 (~48% lower odds)
  • Urban/town/rural mix → OR ≈ 0.89 (~11% lower odds)

4. Academic acceleration remains mobility-aligned.

  • AP participation → OR ≈ 0.77 (~24% lower odds)
  • PSEO → not statistically distinct

Year 10 – Long-Term Workforce Attachment

By ten years, the structure shifts somewhat.

1. CTE remains positive but moderates in magnitude.

  • CTE Concentrator/Completor → OR ≈ 1.25 (~25% higher odds)
  • CTE Participant → OR ≈ 1.19 (~19% higher odds)

CTE remains the strongest consistent positive predictor even a decade later.

2. Structural racial disparities remain large.

  • Black students → OR ≈ 0.51 (~49% lower odds)
  • Asian/PI students → OR ≈ 0.53 (~47% lower odds)
  • American Indian students → OR ≈ 0.57 (~43% lower odds)
  • Hispanic students → OR ≈ 0.71 (~29% lower odds)

Racial gaps are durable across a decade.

3. Urban context remains influential.

  • Entirely urban schools → OR ≈ 0.48 (~52% lower odds)
  • Urban/town/rural mix → ~12% lower odds

4. Local wage context does not meaningfully alter long-term results.

  • County wages → OR ≈ 1.06 (not statistically meaningful)
  • Inclusion of wage context does not attenuate major predictors.

This indicates that by Year 10, sustained regional attachment among non-college graduates is driven primarily by individual preparation and school context rather than county-level wage differences.

Variables that weakened relative to earlier bivariate analyses

  • Academic indicators (ACT, MCA) show limited independent influence once controls are included.
  • PSEO remains statistically non-distinct.
  • Economic disadvantage shows only modest effects relative to race and CTE.
  • Special education status is strongest early and weakens by Year 10.

Overall interpretation

Among non-college graduates, meaningful regional employment is consistently structured by three mechanisms:

  1. Career-aligned preparation (CTE)
  2. Structural racial disparities
  3. Urban school context

CTE is the strongest and most durable positive predictor across 1-, 5-, and 10-year horizons.

Academic acceleration pathways (AP) are consistently associated with lower regional attachment, suggesting mobility alignment rather than retention.

Structural disparities by race and school urbanicity are large, persistent, and not explained by preparation factors.

Unlike the full-population models, local wage context does not independently drive long-term regional retention once individual characteristics are accounted for.

Taken together, this confirms:

  • Within the non-college population, CTE is the central regional workforce anchor.
  • Structural inequalities persist across a decade.
  • School context exerts durable influence.
  • Academic mobility indicators do not translate into regional workforce attachment.


The Story

1. Where do graduates go after high school and college?

The Three Primary Pathways After High School

Graduates follow three broad paths after leaving high school:

  • They enroll in and complete postsecondary education.
  • They enter the workforce without completing a credential.
  • They experience early workforce instability (not meaningfully employed or no Minnesota employment record).

The strongest sorting mechanism in the entire analysis is not whether someone attends college — it is whether and where they complete a credential.

Where College Completers End Up

Among those who complete postsecondary education, geography matters.

Graduating from a postsecondary institution within the region dramatically increases the likelihood of sustained regional employment:

  • 2-year public × Regional graduation → 207% higher odds of sustained regional employment (OR ≈ 3.07)
  • 4-year public × Regional graduation → 142% higher odds (OR ≈ 2.42)

Graduating outside the region strongly predicts employment elsewhere:

  • 4-year public outside MN → ~61% lower odds of sustained regional employment
  • Other sector outside MN → ~62% lower odds

Credential geography overwhelmingly structures workforce geography.

Mobility vs. Anchoring

Academic preparation influences mobility.

Higher ACT and MCA scores are associated with graduating outside the region. Academic strength predicts geographic mobility, not regional anchoring.

Associate degree pathways, especially regional 2-year public institutions, are far more strongly connected to sustained regional employment than bachelor’s pathways.

Non-completers face substantially higher instability risk in the first five years.

2. What are the transition points?

The First Year After Exiting Education

The first year is a major sorting point.

Among non-college graduates:

  • CTE Concentrator/Completor → 42% higher odds of meaningful regional employment in Year 1
  • Special Education status → 29% lower odds
  • Entirely urban high school → 40% lower odds

Early workforce outcomes are not random. They reflect preparation, structural barriers, and school context.

Five-Year Mark: Stabilization or Drift

By Year 5, patterns become durable.

For non-college graduates:

  • CTE Concentrator/Completor → 22–23% higher odds of sustained regional employment
  • Black and Asian/PI students → ~50% lower odds
  • Entirely urban school context → ~48% lower odds

Credential completion geography continues to dominate for college completers.

Ten-Year Mark: Structural Patterns

By Year 10, early academic indicators largely fade, but structural patterns persist.

For non-college graduates:

  • CTE remains positive (≈ 19–25% higher odds)
  • Racial disparities remain large (≈ 43–51% lower odds across groups)
  • Urban school context continues to predict lower regional attachment (~52% lower odds)

Local wage context influences postsecondary graduation location (OR ≈ 1.87), but once workforce attachment is modeled directly among non-college graduates, economic context plays a smaller independent role than preparation and structural factors.

3. What is associated with sustained workforce participation in the region?

The Strongest Predictor: Regional Credential Completion

Across all graduates, the single strongest predictor of sustained regional employment is completing a credential within the region.

  • 2-year public regional graduates → 3.07 odds ratio
  • 4-year public regional graduates → 2.42 odds ratio

Credential location is the primary workforce retention mechanism.

The Workforce-First Anchor: CTE

Among non-college graduates, CTE is the strongest positive independent predictor.

  • Year 1 → up to 42% higher odds
  • Year 5 → ~22–23% higher odds
  • Year 10 → ~19–25% higher odds

CTE remains significant even after controlling for demographics and school context.

Local Economic Ecosystem

Stronger local wage environments are strongly associated with graduating from college in-region:

  • Higher average wages (percent of state) → 87% higher odds of regional graduation (OR ≈ 1.87)

Lower unemployment strengthens workforce attachment.

Economic strength amplifies educational pathways.

Structural Inequality

Structural disparities remain even after controls:

  • Black students → ~49% lower odds of sustained regional employment
  • Asian/PI students → ~36–53% lower odds depending on year
  • American Indian students → ~30–43% lower odds
  • Hispanic students → ~21–29% lower odds
  • Special Education status → substantial early disadvantage

These disparities persist across 1-, 5-, and 10-year horizons.

4. Who experiences persistent workforce instability — and why?

Persistence Is the Norm, Not the Exception

Among non-college graduates, more than half experience non-meaningful employment or no MN employment record in four or more of their first five years after exiting education.

Instability is typically sustained, not temporary.

Early Disconnection Is Sticky

Individuals who fall into non-meaningful employment early are unlikely to rapidly transition into stable attachment.

Recovery happens, but gradual stabilization is more common than quick correction.

Highest Risk Groups

Persistent instability is strongly associated with:

  • Special Education status
  • Economic disadvantage
  • Racial disparities
  • Low CTE exposure
  • Urban school context

These factors consistently predict lower odds of meaningful regional employment.

Regional Underutilization vs. Out-Migration

Most non-meaningful employment among non-college graduates occurs within the region, not outside it.

This is not primarily a brain-drain issue.
It is a regional labor absorption issue.

5. What this means for Central Minnesota

  1. Credential geography drives workforce retention.
    Regional postsecondary completion is the strongest lever for sustained regional employment.

  2. CTE is the strongest workforce stabilizer for non-college graduates.
    Its effects persist across a decade.

  3. Early years matter.
    Year 1 outcomes often set durable trajectories.

  4. Structural disparities persist across the pipeline.
    Race, special education status, and school context influence both graduation and workforce outcomes.

  5. Economic strength amplifies educational pathways.
    Stronger local wage environments increase regional graduation and workforce absorption.

The data show that workforce outcomes in Central Minnesota are shaped by three interacting forces:

  • Educational pathway and credential geography
  • Career-aligned preparation (CTE)
  • Structural and economic context

Retention is not one program or one pathway. It is the intersection of preparation, opportunity, and local labor demand.
