Integrated Summary of Analyses (Final with Non-College Graduates)

Key Findings

Across all analyses, the evidence is clear: postsecondary completion and location of graduation are the strongest predictors of meaningful employment in Central Minnesota.

  • Regional institution graduates and those with an associate degree or lower are most likely to stay employed in the region.
  • Bachelor’s+ degrees often lead to mobility and outmigration.
  • Non-completion predicts the highest risk of disconnection.

The new analysis of non-college graduates highlights that while many struggle to secure stable employment, a subset find meaningful work in the region. Their outcomes are shaped by demographics, local labor market context, and especially CTE participation. This shows that even without postsecondary completion, meaningful regional employment is possible under certain conditions.

Interpretation Over Time and Across Analyses

Demographics

  • Economic disadvantage lowered the likelihood of non-graduates securing meaningful employment, with effects growing stronger 5 and 10 years out.
  • Race/ethnicity disparities persisted, with American Indian and Black non-graduates at higher risk of disconnection.
  • Special education students were less likely to achieve long-term workforce stability.
  • Gender played a smaller role, though male non-graduates had somewhat stronger ties to regional employment in certain industries.

High School Characteristics {unnumbered=“” .unlisted=““}

  • Rural non-graduates were more likely to stay employed in the region, benefiting from proximity to local industries.
  • Urban/town non-graduates showed more mobility and faced higher risks of disconnection when they did not complete college.
  • Local economic conditions (unemployment and relative wages) shaped outcomes: stronger labor markets helped non-graduates sustain meaningful employment locally.

High School Accomplishments

  • CTE participation was the strongest protective factor for non-graduates. Concentrators and completers consistently had higher rates of meaningful employment across 1, 5, and 10 years.
  • PSEO participation among non-graduates was associated with weaker ties to the regional workforce, reflecting a pathway oriented toward bachelor’s completion.
  • Other academic markers (ACT, AP exams) mattered little once a student failed to complete postsecondary.

Postsecondary Pathways (Graduates vs. Non-Graduates)

  • For graduates: regional institutions + associate degrees = strongest anchor to meaningful employment.
  • For non-graduates: outcomes were most positive for CTE-engaged, rural students in strong local labor markets.
  • Non-graduates without CTE or local ties were the most likely to disconnect from the workforce.

CART Analyses

  • For the full population, postsecondary credential and location dominated the decision trees.
  • For non-graduates, CART highlighted CTE engagement, rural school context, and economic status as the best predictors of who stayed meaningfully employed in the region.
  • Academic-prep indicators (PSEO, ACT, AP) did not improve outcomes for non-graduates.

Conclusion

Taken together, the analyses show:

  • Regional college completion with an associate degree or lower remains the most reliable pathway to meaningful employment in Central Minnesota.
  • Non-completers and out-of-state graduates are most at risk of disconnection.
  • For non-college graduates, CTE engagement, rural ties, and favorable local labor markets significantly increase the likelihood of staying employed in the region.

Policy and practice should therefore:

  1. Strengthen CTE pathways in high school as both direct-to-work pipelines and supports for non-college graduates.
  2. Support persistence and completion at regional institutions, especially associate-level programs tied to local industry.
  3. Address inequities faced by economically disadvantaged students, students of color, and special education students, who remain more vulnerable to disconnection.
  4. Leverage rural strengths by expanding partnerships between regional colleges, CTE programs, and local employers.

The expanded analysis underscores that while postsecondary completion is the strongest predictor overall, non-college graduates with CTE experience and rural ties represent an important, often overlooked workforce pipeline for Central Minnesota.


Summaries of each analysis

Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA)

Key Findings

The MCA was used to detect redundancy among categorical variables and determine which could be removed, collapsed, or retained for modeling. Most variables showed distinct contributions across dimensions, indicating low redundancy. However, a few categories had low frequencies or weak contributions, making them candidates for elimination or consolidation. Specifically, Limited English Proficiency, Homelessness, and SAT participation were eliminated due to extremely low representation. Post-secondary variables, particularly ps.grad.InstitutionSector and highest.cred.level, showed overlapping or fragmented categories and were collapsed to simplify interpretation.

Interpretation Across Variable Groups

Demographics: All core variables (gender, race/ethnicity, economic status, English learner, etc.) were retained. Dimensions captured variation around language barriers, COVID graduation year, special education needs, and racial identity. No elimination was recommended.

High School Characteristics: Both RUCA descriptors and economic development region (EDR) captured complementary patterns of rural versus urban schooling context. Both were retained.

High School Accomplishments: ACT/AP participation, PSEO, and CTE engagement represented distinct patterns of academic and vocational achievement. All were retained, though AP exam contributions were weaker.

Post-secondary: Strongest differentiation came from whether students attended, persisted, or graduated from post-secondary institutions. Institutional sector categories and highest credential earned showed fragmentation. Categories for smaller institution types and higher-degree levels were collapsed for interpretability.

Conclusion

The MCA showed that most variables capture unique and meaningful dimensions of variation, but some simplification was needed to avoid overfitting or noise in predictive models. The cleaned dataset removed low-frequency categories, collapsed overlapping post-secondary categories, and retained the full breadth of demographic, high school, and accomplishment variables. This positions the dataset for streamlined, efficient modeling of pathways after high school without losing critical distinctions that drive workforce outcomes.


Local Employment Cross-tabs

Key Findings

Across all analytic sections, postsecondary experiences (graduation, location of graduation, and credential level) emerge as the strongest predictors of meaningful employment in the region. Demographics and high school characteristics show weaker associations, though economic disadvantage, race/ethnicity, and rural/urban school context play notable roles. High school accomplishments such as CTE engagement, ACT/AP exams, and PSEO participation are moderately related but less predictive than postsecondary outcomes.

Interpretation by Section

Demographics

Findings show that gender, economic status, race/ethnicity, and special education needs each have small but significant associations with regional employment outcomes. COVID-era graduates showed different patterns of employment, reflecting disruptions to labor markets and postsecondary pathways. Language-related indicators (non-English home language, English learner) captured additional variation tied to long-term employment challenges. Overall, demographic factors matter but do not dominate predictions.

High School Characteristics

The rural–urban context of the high school (RUCA description, EDR) and local labor conditions (unemployment rate, relative wages) influenced employment outcomes. Rural graduates were more likely to remain in-region but faced limited wage growth, while urban/town graduates showed more geographic mobility. These variables reinforced the importance of context but did not by themselves strongly predict disconnection.

High School Accomplishments

Participation in CTE pathways, PSEO, and standardized testing (ACT, AP) showed distinct though moderate contributions. CTE concentrators and completers were more tied to regional employment, while PSEO and AP takers leaned toward continued postsecondary education and higher mobility. ACT participation, nearly universal, provided less differentiation. These variables reflect educational engagement patterns but were weaker predictors than postsecondary outcomes.

Postsecondary Experience

Postsecondary outcomes provided the clearest distinctions. Graduating from a college in the same region or elsewhere in Minnesota was strongly linked to remaining employed in-state. Graduating outside Minnesota correlated with higher outmigration and disconnection. Credential level was critical: associate and bachelor’s degrees were associated with stable employment, while lack of postsecondary graduation carried a strong risk of disconnection. Institution sector mattered less after collapsing categories, but public 2-year and 4-year pathways aligned closely with regional employment retention.

Conclusion

The cross-tab analysis demonstrates that while demographics and high school factors shape early opportunities, postsecondary experiences—especially location of graduation and credential earned—are the decisive factors for meaningful employment in Central Minnesota. This suggests that workforce engagement strategies should emphasize supporting students to persist through postsecondary programs, with particular attention to encouraging completion within the region. High school initiatives (CTE, PSEO, AP/ACT) serve as important on-ramps but their long-term impact is mediated by what happens in postsecondary education.


Post-secondary Pathways

Key Findings

Across all models, the most consistent predictors of postsecondary engagement and success were economic status, race/ethnicity, CTE engagement, and high school academic accomplishments. Postsecondary graduation and location of graduation emerged as critical determinants of meaningful employment in Central Minnesota. Early postsecondary attendance (within one year of high school) and eventual completion strongly correlated with stable workforce participation.

Interpretation by Postsecondary Pathway

Attended Postsecondary Within One Year of High School Graduation

Graduates who attended within one year showed stronger long-term labor market outcomes. Economic disadvantage and special education status reduced the likelihood of early attendance, while higher ACT scores, PSEO participation, and AP exams increased it. Race/ethnicity also influenced early attendance, with White and Asian students participating at higher rates than Black, Hispanic, or American Indian peers. CTE concentrators were somewhat less likely to enroll immediately but more tied to regional employment when they did.

Attended Postsecondary (Ever)

Broadly attending postsecondary, regardless of timing, was shaped by similar predictors: academic readiness (ACT, AP, PSEO) increased attendance; economic disadvantage, special education, and being from rural high schools decreased it. CTE engagement was more complex, with concentrators more tied to regional employment but less likely to pursue postsecondary broadly compared to peers who leaned toward academic pathways.

Graduated Postsecondary (ps.grad)

Completion was the strongest predictor of meaningful regional employment. Credential level mattered: associate and bachelor’s degrees were most associated with stable employment, while non-completion posed high risk of disconnection. Demographic disparities were stark: economically disadvantaged students, English learners, and students of color faced lower graduation rates. High school accomplishments again showed predictive value: ACT/AP participation, strong MCA scores, and PSEO were linked to higher completion. CTE concentrators connected more strongly to employment outcomes when paired with completion, especially at 2-year institutions.

Conclusion

The analysis of postsecondary pathways confirms that completion and location of graduation are the most powerful drivers of workforce outcomes in Central Minnesota. Early enrollment and steady progress increase completion odds, but inequities by race, income, and educational background persist. High school accomplishments such as ACT, AP, PSEO, and CTE play meaningful roles, but their effects are mediated by whether students complete a credential. Policy and practice interventions should therefore prioritize postsecondary persistence and completion supports, particularly for disadvantaged and rural students, while leveraging CTE and local institutions to strengthen regional workforce retention.


CART — Predictors of Regional Employment

Key Findings

The CART analysis identified postsecondary outcomes as the strongest predictors of meaningful employment in Central Minnesota. Specifically, credential level and location of postsecondary graduation consistently appeared at the top of decision trees. Demographic and high school variables (economic status, race/ethnicity, special education, CTE engagement) provided secondary differentiation but were far less predictive. Model accuracy was stable across years (~65–70%), with pruning improving interpretability without major loss of accuracy.

Interpretation Over Time

One Year After Graduation

The most decisive factors were whether an individual attended postsecondary and their immediate enrollment pathway. Early postsecondary participation often split outcomes, with non-attenders at higher risk of disconnection. Demographic characteristics (economic disadvantage, special education) played a role in refining predictions within these groups, but credential attainment had not yet fully separated outcomes.

Five Years After Graduation

Credential completion began to dominate predictions. Associate and bachelor’s degree earners were consistently linked with meaningful employment in the region, while non-completers were more often disconnected. Location of graduation also became more predictive: students completing degrees within the region or Minnesota were more likely to stay employed in-state, while those graduating elsewhere showed greater outmigration.

Ten Years After Graduation

Postsecondary variables fully overshadowed earlier predictors. Highest credential earned and location of postsecondary completion were decisive. Demographics and high school accomplishments played minimal roles, suggesting that long-term employment outcomes are overwhelmingly structured by postsecondary success and geography of graduation.

Conclusion

CART modeling confirms that meaningful employment in the region is most strongly driven by postsecondary pathways, particularly credential completion and location of graduation. Demographics and high school accomplishments matter in shaping who reaches postsecondary, but they fade in predictive importance over time. For policy and practice, this underscores the need to:

  1. Support postsecondary persistence and completion,
  2. Strengthen regional institutions as anchors for retaining graduates locally, and
  3. Address early barriers that limit access to these decisive pathways.


CART — Postsecondary Paths

Key Findings

The CART analysis was designed to identify which high school characteristics, accomplishments, and demographics increase the likelihood that students will graduate from a regional institution or with an associate-level credential, since those pathways are most strongly tied to meaningful employment in Central Minnesota.

The models highlight that CTE engagement, economic background, and certain demographic factors were the most consistent predictors of these pathways. In contrast, students with strong academic signals such as ACT/AP/PSEO participation were more likely to pursue bachelor’s degrees outside the region, making them less likely to end up in meaningful regional employment.

Interpretation

Postsecondary Graduation Location

Graduating from a regional institution was more common among students who had participated in career and technical education (CTE) during high school. Economic disadvantage and rural school context also increased the probability of choosing a regional college. By contrast, academically advanced students (ACT/AP/PSEO) tended to cluster toward institutions outside the region, reducing their likelihood of local employment.

Highest Credential Level

Earning an associate degree or lower was strongly tied to high school CTE concentration or completion. These students had clear pathways into local industries and showed higher retention in the regional workforce. In contrast, students whose high school profiles emphasized college preparatory routes (ACT, AP exams, PSEO) were more likely to earn bachelor’s degrees, which often led them out of the region.

Conclusion

The CART analysis shows that high school CTE engagement and regional economic context are key drivers of pathways that lead to meaningful employment in Central Minnesota. Students who take CTE courses and graduate from regional institutions with associate-level credentials are most likely to stay and contribute to the local workforce. Meanwhile, students prepared for bachelor’s or higher credentials often migrate out of the region.

This suggests workforce strategies should:

  1. Strengthen and expand CTE pathways in high school as feeders into regional colleges and local employment,
  2. Build stronger connections between regional colleges and local employers to retain associate-level graduates, and
  3. Acknowledge the mobility of bachelor’s-bound students, while still creating incentives for them to return or connect to regional labor markets.


Non-college graduates and local employment

Cross-tabs

For non-college graduates, the central question is whether they secure meaningful employment in their home region. The evidence shows that regional employment is influenced most strongly by students’ accomplishments during high school, with demographics and local economic conditions playing secondary roles.

High school accomplishments stand out as the clearest drivers of regional employment. Career and Technical Education (CTE) is especially powerful: concentrators and students who take more CTE courses are consistently more likely to find meaningful regional work, and this advantage endures 10 years after graduation. Academic performance, measured by ACT and MCA scores, also matters. Lower scorers are more likely to transition earlier into the workforce, often finding employment in their home region. Higher scorers, by contrast, tend to pursue postsecondary education and eventually secure meaningful work elsewhere in Minnesota. PSEO participation provides an early push toward persistence and statewide opportunities, but it reduces the likelihood of staying in regional employment over time.

Demographics create important differences in access to regional work. Students in special education are far less likely to achieve meaningful regional employment and are instead overrepresented in non-meaningful work or disconnection. Economic status also plays a role: students from higher-income backgrounds are more likely to move into statewide opportunities, while lower-income peers are somewhat more concentrated in regional jobs, but also at higher risk of disconnection. Gender differences are small but consistent, with men more likely to be in meaningful regional jobs and women more often outside the regional workforce. Race and ethnicity show very modest overall effects; white graduates are slightly more likely to secure regional jobs compared to peers of color.

High school characteristics and local context shape the structure of opportunities available. Rural graduates are more likely to remain in meaningful regional employment, while urban graduates are more likely to move into Minnesota-wide jobs. County-level conditions matter: stronger local economies with lower unemployment and higher wages increase the chances that graduates find meaningful regional employment, while weaker counties increase the likelihood of disconnection. Regional (EDR) differences exist but are comparatively modest.

Taken together, the story is clear: for non-college graduates, CTE engagement is the most reliable path into meaningful employment in the region. Lower academic scorers and rural graduates are also more likely to remain rooted in local work, while higher academic achievers and those with early postsecondary exposure often leave the region for broader Minnesota opportunities. Demographics highlight groups at risk of missing out on regional work, and county conditions reveal where local economies either anchor graduates in meaningful jobs or push them toward disconnection.

Ranked Importance for Regional Employment (Most → Least)

  1. CTE Engagement (Accomplishments: achievement + total courses) – strongest, most consistent predictor of meaningful regional work.
  2. ACT & MCA Scores (Accomplishments) – lower scorers more likely to enter and remain in regional jobs; higher scorers more likely to leave the region.
  3. Special Education Status (Demographic) – persistent disadvantage, much less likely to secure regional work.
  4. Economic Status (Demographic) – lower-income grads somewhat more region-bound but also more disconnected; higher-income grads more mobile across MN.
  5. PSEO Participation (Accomplishment) – reduces likelihood of regional employment over time as participants pursue statewide opportunities.
  6. Rural/Urban (HS Characteristic) – rural grads more likely to stay in regional jobs; urban grads more likely to find work elsewhere in Minnesota.
  7. County Conditions (Unemployment, Wages) – stronger local economies increase chances of regional employment.
  8. Gender (Demographic) – men more likely in regional work; women more often outside.
  9. Region (EDR) (HS Characteristic) – modest variation across Minnesota.
  10. Race/Ethnicity (Demographic) – very small effects on regional employment.

👉 The headline story becomes: Among non-college graduates, the most dependable route into meaningful employment in the region is deep engagement with CTE. Regional jobs are also more common for lower scorers, rural students, and men, while higher scorers, PSEO participants, and higher-income students are more likely to move into statewide opportunities instead. Local economic conditions either anchor graduates in meaningful regional work or push them toward disconnection.

CART

Regional vs. MN (Mobility vs. Staying Local)

When comparing meaningful regional employment to meaningful employment elsewhere in Minnesota, the CART models show that:

  • Early (Year 1): Outcomes are shaped by rural/urban context, local wages, economic status, and CTE achievement. Rural grads and CTE concentrators are anchored in regional work, while urban, higher-income, and non-CTE students are more mobile.
  • Mid-term (Year 5): ACT scores emerge as a driver of statewide mobility. Higher scorers are more likely to leave the region, while lower scorers remain. Structural anchors like wages and rural roots continue to matter.
  • Long-term (Year 10): Local economic conditions dominate. Low wages and low unemployment tie students to regional jobs, while higher wages and unemployment drive mobility. Individual student characteristics fade.

Core Story: Regional vs. MN employment is primarily about mobility and opportunity-seeking. Early on, CTE and rural roots anchor students locally, but by 10 years, local labor markets alone decide whether students stay or leave.

Regional vs. Not Meaningful (Success vs. Disconnection)

When comparing meaningful regional employment to non-meaningful work (not attending postsecondary), the CART models show that:

  • Early (Year 1): CTE achievement is the strongest determinant. Concentrators with more courses are far more likely to be employed regionally, while others fall into non-meaningful work. Economic status and local conditions reinforce these divides.
  • Mid-term (Year 5): The picture broadens to include special education status, rural/urban context, and income. CTE remains protective, but special education is a critical barrier.
  • Long-term (Year 10): Structural conditions (wages, unemployment) dominate. CTE continues to provide some protection, but special education remains a barrier even a decade later.

Core Story: Regional vs. not meaningful employment is about who is prepared and supported to succeed. CTE provides consistent protection, but special education and weak economies increase risk of long-term disconnection.

Regional vs. No MN Record (Presence vs. Absence)

When comparing meaningful regional employment to having no Minnesota employment record, the CART models show that:

  • Early (Year 1): CTE course-taking (≥3) is the main anchor into regional employment. Special education status and ACT scores also matter: lower ACT scorers are more likely to stay, while higher scorers are more mobile or missing from records.
  • Mid-term (Year 5): Anchors broaden. CTE remains protective, but rural context, income, and MCA Reading scores now also help explain who sustains regional employment. Lower ACT scorers continue to be more regionally anchored.
  • Long-term (Year 10): The balance flips, with most students having no MN record. By this stage, county wages dominate, and CTE achievement/heavy course-taking remain the key educational anchor. Rural settings still offer modest protection.

Core Story: Regional vs. no MN record is about labor market attachment vs. disconnection from Minnesota altogether. Early on, preparation (CTE) and mobility (ACT) explain outcomes, but by 10 years, economic strength and CTE determine who remains visible in the state workforce.

Big Picture Takeaways

  • CTE is the most consistent protective factor across all comparisons. Heavy CTE exposure anchors students into regional work, reducing both disconnection and mobility.
  • Special education status is a persistent barrier for avoiding non-meaningful outcomes, but less central for mobility (vs. MN) or long-term presence (vs. no record).
  • ACT scores work differently across contexts: high scorers are more mobile (regional vs. MN) and more likely missing from records (regional vs. no record).
  • Rural context is a consistent anchor for staying local, while urban/town settings increase risk of both mobility and disconnection.
  • Economic structure dominates by Year 10. Wages and unemployment are the strongest determinants of whether non-college graduates sustain regional employment, outcompeting most student-level factors.

Final Synthesis:
Across all outcomes, the story is consistent: early regional employment is shaped by individual preparation (especially CTE), but long-term sustainability is determined by local economic strength. Policymakers should view CTE as an early protective tool and local labor market investment as the long-term solution for retaining non-college graduates in meaningful regional work.
