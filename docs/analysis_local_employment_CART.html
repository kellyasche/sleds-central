Rank of predictors

Our analysis of local employment using cross-tabs, Kramer v-test, and ANOVA tables, we identified the following rank of importance regarding independent variable relationships to employment outcomes after high school;

1. Strong Predictors of Regional Employment and Disconnection

These variables consistently show strong or moderate Cramer’s V values and clear differences in meaningful employment in the region, making them strong candidates for inclusion in modeling.

Variable Type Key Insights
ps.grad.location Postsecondary Strongest predictor of regional employment. Local grads more likely to stay.
ps.grad Postsecondary Graduation itself reduces disconnection and improves employment outcomes.
highest.cred.level Postsecondary Clear stepwise value: higher credential = better outcomes.
ps.grad.InstitutionSector Postsecondary 2-year grads more likely to stay regional; 4-year grads more mobile.

2. Moderate Predictors — Some Association, Less Consistency

These variables show moderate associations with outcomes. Their predictive value may depend on how they interact with other variables and could be useful in modeling, especially when looking for cumulative effects.

These variables showed very weak associations (low Cramer’s V, minimal group differences) and could be considered for dropping from modeling, unless interaction effects suggest otherwise.

Employment states

Currently, we have the following employment states for 1-, 5- and 10-years after graduating high school.

  • Meaningful emp region: Individual has meaningful employment in the region of their high school.
  • Meaningful emp MN: Individual has meaningful employment in Minnesota, but not in the region of their high school.
  • Attending ps: Individual is attending post-secondary and does not have meaningful employment.
  • Not meaningful, not attending ps: Individual is working in Minnesota, but it is not meaningful and is not attending post-secondary.
  • No MN record, not attending ps: Individual does not have a MN employment record and is not attending post-secondary.


Methodology

This study investigates the factors that influence whether high school graduates achieve meaningful employment in the same region as their high school at 1, 5, and 10 years after graduation. To isolate and understand the predictors of this specific outcome, the analysis compares it separately against three alternative post-graduation statuses:

  1. Meaningful employment in Minnesota, but outside the region
  2. Not meaningful employment in Minnesota and not attending post-secondary
  3. No Minnesota employment record and not attending post-secondary

Graduates classified as Attending post-secondary at the time of measurement are excluded from the analysis, as their employment pathways are distinct and not directly comparable to full-time workforce participation.

For each of the three alternative statuses and each time point (1, 5, and 10 years after graduation), a separate binary classification model is created, resulting in nine total models. This binary framework ensures that “Meaningful employment in the region” is always the positive class, allowing a direct focus on distinguishing regional retention from each alternative pathway.

The modeling process follows these steps:

  1. Data Preparation All predictor variables include demographic characteristics, high school achievement indicators, and postsecondary attainment measures. Records with missing target outcome categories are excluded. For each binary comparison, the dataset is filtered to only include the two relevant classes, with “Meaningful employment in the region” coded as the positive class.

  2. Training and Test Split For each binary dataset, the data is randomly split into a training set (70–80%) and a testing set (20–30%) to ensure that model evaluation is conducted on previously unseen data.

  3. Preprocessing Preprocessing steps are applied to the training data using a recipe in the tidymodels framework:

    • Remove near-zero variance predictors.
    • Impute missing values for categorical predictors with the mode and numeric predictors with the median.
    • Encode categorical predictors as dummy variables.
    • Upsample the minority class to balance class representation.

  4. Modeling

    Two machine learning methods are applied to each binary comparison:

    • CART (Classification and Regression Tree) for interpretability and identification of clear decision rules.
    • Random Forest for potentially higher predictive performance and more robust variable importance estimation.

  5. Evaluation Models are evaluated on the testing set using accuracy, Cohen’s kappa, balanced accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Variable importance is examined to identify the most influential predictors for each comparison and time point.

This approach emphasizes clarity in interpretation by focusing each model on a single binary distinction, making it easier to identify which factors most strongly differentiate graduates who achieve meaningful regional employment from those on alternative pathways.

Overall Performance

  • Accuracy (61.4%) – The model is correct in a little over six out of ten predictions, which is worse than simply guessing the majority class (“Meaningful emp region”, 63.4%). This is why the p-value for Acc > NIR is ~1, meaning there’s no statistically significant improvement over the naive baseline.
  • Kappa (0.139) – Very low agreement beyond chance; the model isn’t capturing much true discriminatory power between the two groups.

Class-Level Performance (with “Meaningful emp region” as the positive class):

  • Sensitivity (74.2%) – The model is fairly good at identifying actual “Meaningful emp region” cases.
  • Specificity (39.3%) – The model is poor at correctly identifying “Meaningful emp MN” cases; it confuses many of them for “Meaningful emp region.”
  • Positive Predictive Value (68.0%) – When the model predicts “Meaningful emp region,” it’s correct about two-thirds of the time.
  • Negative Predictive Value (46.7%) – When the model predicts “Meaningful emp MN,” it’s correct less than half the time.

Balanced Accuracy (56.7%)

  • This average of sensitivity and specificity shows the model is performing only slightly better than random guessing for this binary classification.

Interpretation Your CART model in this configuration is over-predicting “Meaningful emp region” and struggling to correctly identify “Meaningful emp MN” cases. This is reflected in the large difference between sensitivity (~74%) and specificity (~39%).

In practical terms, this means your model catches most of the “Meaningful emp region” group, but at the cost of misclassifying a lot of “Meaningful emp MN” students.


Pre-pruning

Next, we need to prune. We can either pre-prune or post-prune. We will start with pre-pruning and use each method - max depth, min depth, and min bucket. It’s recommended that I start with the following parameters;

  • maxdepth: 6
  • minsplit: 5% of nrow(train) which is 928.25.
  • minbucket: round(minsplit / 3) which is 309


maxdepth = 6

minsplit = .05*nrow(train)

minbucket = round(minsplit / 3)

meaningful.emp.mn.grad.year.1.model.preprune <- rpart(grad.year.1 ~ ., data = train, method = "class", 
                   control = rpart.control(cp = 0, maxdepth = maxdepth, minsplit = minsplit, minbucket = minbucket))

test$pred <- predict(meaningful.emp.mn.grad.year.1.model.preprune, test, type = "class")

Next, let’s try postpruning. The idea here is to allow the decision tree to grow fully and observe the CP value. There are two methods for identifying the CP code;

  1. Use the cp value with the lowest xerror from the base model.
  2. Use the cp value with the next lowest xerror that is within the standard error.


cp_table <- meaningful.emp.mn.grad.year.1.model$cptable

min_xerror <- min(cp_table[, "xerror"])
min_xerror_se <- cp_table[which.min(cp_table[,"xerror"]), "xstd"]

threshold <- min_xerror + min_xerror_se
